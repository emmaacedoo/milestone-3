Introduction

I decided to study the Olympics because I really like sports and I am interested in comparing the United States with European countries. I want to see who has won more medals, which sports different countries are best at, and how Olympic results have changed over time. I also want to focus a little more on Spain because I am from there.

This project builds on my Milestone 2 work using the 120 Years of Olympic History: Athletes and Results dataset from Kaggle. The dataset contains 271,116 rows and 15 columns, covering both Summer and Winter Olympic Games from 1896 to 2016. Because the dataset ends in 2016, more recent Olympic Games (such as Tokyo 2020 or Paris 2024) are not included in this analysis.

Variables Used in This Project

To explore Olympic performance across countries and over time, I use the following key variables from the dataset:

  • Year: to see how Olympic results and medal counts change over time from 1896 to 2016.
  • NOC and Team: to identify countries using their official National Olympic Committee (NOC) 3-letter codes and team names.
  • Sport and Event: to compare different sports (like Swimming, Athletics, and Sailing) and individual medal events.
  • Medal: to count Gold, Silver, and Bronze medals won.
  • Sex: to explore differences in participation and medal counts between male (M) and female (F) athletes.
  • Age: to explore athletes’ ages across regions and sports.

Methodology: Counting Medals Fairly & Defining Europe

Two important choices make our comparison accurate and fair:

  1. Counting unique event medals instead of athlete rows: In the raw dataset, every player on a winning basketball, soccer, or rowing team gets their own row. If we simply counted rows, a single basketball gold medal would count as 12 medals! To avoid overcounting team sports, I use distinct(Year, Season, Games, Sport, Event, NOC, Medal) so that each country receives one medal per event.
  2. Consistent European Selection (12 Countries): Throughout all charts, I use a clearly defined set of 12 European Olympic countries: Spain (ESP), Great Britain (GBR), France (FRA), Germany (GER), Italy (ITA), Sweden (SWE), Norway (NOR), Netherlands (NED), Hungary (HUN), Finland (FIN), Switzerland (SUI), and Poland (POL).

Why Group vs. Individual Comparisons Matter: Comparing the United States (2,823 medals) against 12 European countries combined (6,849 medals) is very different from comparing individual countries. In the Olympics, each European nation sends its own full delegation of athletes and teams, so combining 12 countries naturally produces a larger group total. However, when we compare individual countries one-on-one, the United States (2,823 medals) has won nearly three times as many medals as the highest-ranking European country in our group, Germany (1,004), followed by Great Britain (919) and France (879).

1. USA vs. Europe: Olympic Medals Over Time (1896–2016)

This first interactive chart tracks total unique event medals won in each Olympic year from 1896 to 2016 (combining Summer and Winter Games held in the same year). You can hover over any point to see the exact year and medal total.

Notice how the 12 European countries combined usually win more medals as a group, while the United States alone reaches massive peaks (such as 1904 in St. Louis and 1984 in Los Angeles) and competes closely with an entire continent’s worth of teams.

# Count unique event medals by Year for the USA vs. the 12 selected European countries
medals_by_year <- medals_unique |>
  filter(NOC %in% c("USA", euro_nocs)) |> # Keep USA and selected European NOCs
  mutate(
    Group = if_else(
      NOC == "USA",
      "United States (USA)",
      "Selected Europe (12 Countries Combined)"
    )
  ) |>
  count(Year, Group, name = "Medals") # Count unique medals per year and group

# Create the ggplot2 time-series line chart
p1 <- ggplot(medals_by_year, aes(x = Year, y = Medals, color = Group)) +
  geom_line(linewidth = 1.1) + # Draw trend lines over time

      "Selected Europe (12 Countries Combined)" = "#a855f7",

  scale_x_continuous(breaks = seq(1896, 2016, by = 16)) +

3. Focus on Spain: Olympic Medal History (1900–2016)

Because I am from Spain (ESP), I wanted to take a closer look at how Spain’s Olympic performance has evolved over time. Through 2016, Spain has won 150 unique event medals (45 Gold, 63 Silver, and 42 Bronze), with its top sports being Sailing (19 medals), Canoeing (16), Cycling (15), Athletics (14), and Tennis (12).

As the chart below clearly shows, 1992 was the turning point in Spanish Olympic history. When Barcelona hosted the 1992 Summer Olympics, Spain invested heavily in athlete training and won 23 total medals in 1992 (22 in Summer—including 13 Golds—and 1 Bronze in Winter). Since 1992, Spain has remained a strong Olympic contender at every Summer Games through 2016.

# Filter unique medals for Spain (ESP) and order Medal factor levels

Conclusion & Data Source

Looking at 120 years of Olympic data from 1896 to 2016, the answer to “Who dominates the Olympics?” depends on how we frame the comparison:

  • As a group of 12 countries, our selected European nations have won more combined unique event medals (6,849) than the United States (2,823), largely because 12 separate countries can enter many more athletes and teams across every sport, especially in Cycling, Fencing, Canoeing, and Rowing.
  • As a single country, the United States is by far the most dominant individual nation in Olympic history, winning nearly triple the medals of any single European country and even beating all 12 European countries combined in Swimming.
  • Spain (ESP) transformed its Olympic program starting with the 1992 Barcelona Olympics, growing from winning only a few medals per Games before 1992 to consistently winning between 11 and 20 medals at every Summer Olympics through 2016.
